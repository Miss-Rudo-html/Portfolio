LIGHTING & SOUND

  • The ERS and Fresnel do completely different things, and I didn't really get that until I started using them. The ERS throws a tight, focused beam you can actually shape. The Fresnel spreads a soft wash across the whole area. Getting that difference right changed how I thought about every scene.
  • Where you place a light matters as much as which light you pick. The same fixture at leg height can make someone look threatening, at chest height they look completely normal, and from directly behind they just become a silhouette. I never expected position to change things that much.
  • Everything in a lighting system connects to the next piece — the dimmer rack powers the fixtures, the board runs the dimmer rack, channels keep the board organized, and cables tie it all together. Once I mapped that out in my head, I actually knew what I was doing when something went wrong.
  • Writing a cue sheet meant thinking about light as timing, not just design. Every cue had to land at a specific moment, and being even a second off changes what the audience feels during that scene.
  • Calling cues live was nothing like writing them on paper. I had to stay locked in on the script, keep track of what was happening onstage, and hit each cue at exactly the right moment — no pausing, no second-guessing, no catching up.

"Before this assignment, a lighting rig was just a bunch of metal hanging above the stage to me. The Lighting Textbook changed that. Going through every piece one by one — the ERS with its tight, shapeable beam, the Fresnel's soft spread, the way a single gel completely shifts a scene's mood — I started seeing the whole system as something that actually makes sense. Every part connects to the next. The dimmer rack feeds the fixtures, the board runs the dimmer rack, channels keep the board organized, cables carry it all. Finding out that a two-fer splits one circuit into two outputs, or that a top hat can cut down a messy spill, made it clear that theatrical lighting is just as much about solving problems as it is about design."

GoboA shaped metal or glass template that slots into an ERS to project a pattern — like a web, branches, or stars — onto whatever surface you point the beam at.
Pin ConnectorThe standard stage electrical connector for linking fixtures to dimmer circuits. Sturdy, simple, and used basically everywhere in a theatrical rig.
Top HatA cylindrical tube that slides onto the front of a fixture to cut down light spill and stop the beam from spreading where it shouldn't.
ChannelA numbered slot on the lighting board that controls the intensity of whatever fixtures are assigned to it. How the board knows which light to talk to.
StriplightsLong fixtures with a row of bulbs that throw a wide, even wash of color across a backdrop or cyc.
GelA thin sheet of colored plastic that goes in front of a fixture to tint the beam. Simple, cheap, and completely changes the look of a light.
Dimmer RackA rack of dimmers that controls how much power goes to each lighting circuit — which is how you control brightness.
ParnelA hybrid of a PAR and a Fresnel. You get a medium spread that's softer than an ERS but more focused than a pure Fresnel wash.
Two-FerA Y-shaped connector that splits one dimmer circuit into two outputs, so two fixtures can run off the same channel.
CableStandard stage wiring that carries power from the dimmer rack to the fixtures, usually terminated with stage pin or Edison connectors.
ScoopA basic bowl-shaped fixture that throws a wide, soft wash with no hard edge — good for general coverage or color fills.
Channel BoardThe panel where channels get patched and assigned, connecting each fixture to the dimmer circuit that controls it.
Lighting BoardThe main console where cues get programmed, stored, and run during a show. Everything lives here.
ERSEllipsoidal Reflector Spotlight — the most common fixture in a theatre rig. Throws a sharp, controllable beam you can shape with shutters and accepts gobos.
FresnelA fixture with a stepped glass lens that produces a soft, adjustable beam with no hard edge. Good for washing an area with even, diffused light.

TOOL MUSEUM

  • Every tool in the shop has one job it does well. Using the wrong one either makes the work harder or damages the material — sometimes both. Figuring that out early on changed how I set up before starting anything.
  • Knowing a tool's name is just the beginning. I had to learn how to hold it, what the right technique looks like, and what not to do — because doing it wrong either ruins what you're working on or puts someone at risk.
  • Power tools need to be set up correctly before you even turn them on. The bit, the depth, the angle — all of that has to be right first, or the cut comes out wrong and you end up fixing mistakes instead of moving forward.
  • Hand tools mattered just as much as the power ones. The speed square, chalk line, and clamps were what kept everything measured and steady. Without those, none of the precision would have held up.
  • The shop runs better when everyone takes care of it. Putting tools back in the right place after every use sounds simple, but it actually keeps the whole class moving — not just you.
assignment Tool Identification & Museum ▾

"The Tool Museum had us photograph and identify every hand tool in the shop. Going through each one — not just what it's called, but how to hold it, the technique behind using it, and what not to do — made me realize how much practical knowledge is packed into a working shop. Knowing a tool by name and actually knowing how to use it are two completely different things."

C-ClampA screw-down clamp shaped like a C, used to hold pieces together while glue sets or while you're nailing. You tighten the screw until the jaw bites in.
Chalk LineA reel of chalk-coated string that you snap against a surface to leave a perfectly straight line. Essential for marking long distances that a ruler can't cover in one shot.
HammerThe most basic tool in the shop. Drives nails — but getting the swing right so the nail goes in straight without splitting the wood is something you actually have to learn.
Cordless Drill / DriverA battery-powered drill and driver in one. Swap the bit to switch between boring holes and driving screws without changing tools entirely.
Vice GripLocking pliers that clamp onto something and stay clamped without you holding them — useful when you need both hands free but still need something held in place.
Chop SawA power saw that drops straight down for a clean, fast crosscut. The main go-to for cutting lumber to exact lengths.
Speed SquareA small triangular tool for marking 90° and 45° cut lines, and checking corners are actually square before you commit to fastening.
Bar ClampA long clamp that applies even pressure across a wide area — used to hold flat frames together while you fasten them.
Adjustable WrenchA wrench with a jaw that adjusts to fit bolts and nuts of different sizes. Useful for tightening hardware on set pieces and rigging.
Set ScrewA small threaded fastener with no head — tightened with an Allen key. It locks one piece against another from the inside, with nothing sticking out.
Pneumatic Nail GunAn air-powered gun that drives nails in one pull of the trigger. Much faster than swinging a hammer, and keeps the depth consistent every time.

SCENIC ART

  • Scenic painting is nothing like painting at home. Brushstrokes that look rough and messy up close read completely fine from 30 feet away. I had to stop judging my work from inches away and start checking it from across the room.
  • Faux finishes aren't about accuracy — they're about convincing someone fast. The goal isn't to copy brick exactly, it's to get someone sitting in the house to instantly read "brick" without thinking about it. That shift changed how I approached every technique.
  • Paint consistency mattered more than I expected. Too thick and the brush drags. Too thin and it bleeds across the surface. Each technique needs a specific mix to work, and I had to get that right before touching the flat or the whole thing would fall apart.
  • Washes made the biggest visual difference. A dark wash pushed into the recessed areas created instant shadow and depth. A light wash on the raised parts lifted the highlights. It was the simplest technique and the one that made everything look most convincing.
  • Having a sketch or reference before I started saved me more than once. When I got stuck mid-project or started second-guessing a decision, I could look back at the drawing and remember what I was actually trying to do.

assignment Paint Techniques ▾

"This assignment had us practice five scenic painting techniques directly on our flats. Each one is a different way of getting flat wood to read as a real material from a seat in the house — brick, stone, aged plaster. Controlling paint consistency, brush pressure, and layering order was the real skill behind all five of them."

Faux BricksLayered red-brown tones with white mortar lines painted in between, finished with spatter to age the surface. Up close it looks rough; from across a stage it reads as a brick wall.
Faux GraniteA grey base coat with heavy black and white spatter over it. The flecks are what make it read as polished stone.
Faux Wood GrainBrown paint dragged in long strokes with a brush or comb to simulate wood grain. It's all about the direction you pull and the pressure you use.
Faux Plaster PeelTape laid in a geometric pattern, painted over with layered washes, then pulled back to reveal cracked lines underneath — like a wall that's been aging for years.
Faux RocksRounded grey shapes built up with circular scumble strokes, then a shadow wash pushed into the low spots and a highlight wash on the raised parts to give them dimension.

FLATS

  • Building flats showed me this is actual construction — same rules, same tolerances. A frame even half an inch out of square will be visibly wrong onstage every single night of the show.
  • The order you work in isn't flexible. Measure, mark, cut, check square, then fasten — every time, in that order. I learned what happens when you skip the checking step and go straight to fastening, and it took twice as long to fix.
  • The cut list isn't a suggestion. Every piece has a specific length and its own position in the frame, and swapping anything causes problems that build on each other through the whole assembly. I followed it exactly.
  • Faux painting on a flat can't be rushed. Base coat first, technique pass second, shadows and highlights last — and each layer has to be completely dry before the next one goes on. I skipped that once and watched everything blend into a mess.
  • The school bus flat taught me that a set piece can carry something personal. Adding my bus numbers and the Blue Bus circle made a functional flat feel like it actually meant something. Even something built for the stage can have a real story behind it.

PROPS

  • Props get used hard. Every rehearsal, every performance — they get picked up, set down, carried around. I had to build with that kind of use in mind, not just make something that looked good sitting on a shelf.
  • The hobbit house hills came from crumpled parchment paper and tape, and they looked completely convincing. I went in thinking I needed specific materials, and got a better result from stuff that was already around.
  • Having the sketch made the build better. Things changed as I went, but those changes were decisions I made on purpose — not accidents from having nothing to fall back on when something didn't work out.
  • The small details were what made the hobbit house feel finished. The lights inside, the fences around the moss, the stone fountain — those things pushed it past looking like a craft project and made it feel like an actual scene.
  • Photographing each stage of the hobbit house build showed me how much the work changed from start to finish. It also made it easier to see what was still missing before I could actually call it done.

REFLECTION

This year in Tech Theater I worked through thirteen projects that gave me a real picture of what it actually takes to put a production together — from the light board to the prop bench.

The Lighting Textbook and Lighting Shadows assignments were where I first started understanding how to read a rig. Going through each fixture — ERS, Fresnel, Parnel, Scoop — and seeing what angle and position do to how something reads onstage gave me a foundation for everything in the lighting unit. The Lighting Practice Site let me put those ideas to work in a simulator, building five different looks from nothing. The Final Light Cue Sheet pushed that further — writing cues meant thinking about light as timing, where every design decision has a specific moment attached to it. The Prompt Book was where it all came together live: I watched the show recording and called out “warning lights” every time the lights were about to change, learning what it actually feels like to track a performance in real time. None of these five needed any tools — just paying attention and making decisions.

The Tool Identification and Museum assignment introduced me to every tool in the shop — C-Clamp, Chalk Line, Hammer, Cordless Drill, Vice Grip, Chop Saw, Speed Square, Bar Clamp, Adjustable Wrench, Set Screw, and Pneumatic Nail Gun. Learning the correct grip and technique for each one was what made everything that came after actually work.

The Bird House in the Scenic Art section was my first build. I used the Durmax, Chop Saw, Wood Glue, Pneumatic Staple Gun, Paint, and Nail Sets to cut and put it together from scratch. Midway through, right when I was ready to start assembly, I came back to class and my cut pieces were gone — the advanced tech class had either taken or misplaced them. I sorted it out with Mr. Domack, who remade every cut so I could keep going on schedule. I painted it with a sunflower and gave it to my nana.

Cubby Painting was my first purely creative assignment — no template, no requirements, just a blank cubby and paint. I did a lily of the valley, and it was the first time in this class I had full control over what I made.

The Paint Techniques assignment had me practice five faux finishes directly on my flat — Faux Bricks, Faux Granite, Faux Wood Grain, Faux Plaster Peel, and Faux Rocks — using paint only. Each one was a different way of getting flat wood to read as a real material from across a stage. Getting the consistency and layering right is what made any of them actually work.

Flat Construction was where I built the actual flat frame. Using the Chop Saw, Wood Glue, and Pneumatic Staple Gun, I measured, cut, squared, and fastened lumber into a full frame — and found out quickly that a flat half an inch out of square will show onstage every night. The Final Paint project used those same tools plus a Jig Saw. I painted a yellow school bus with personal details: the number 1530 for my high school bus, a blue circle for the elementary school Blue Bus, and 13 for my middle school route. Every school I've been to had a different bus, and I wanted all of them on one flat.

The Birdhouse in Props is the same project as the one in Scenic Art — same tools, same story about the missing wood, same resolution with Mr. Domack stepping in — but it appears in Props as well because the finished piece functions as a prop.

Finally, the Hobbit House was the biggest project of the year. Using Paper, Tape, Hot Glue, Cardstock, Paper Mache, Paint, Foam, and Scissors, I built a miniature scene from scratch — starting with a sketch and working through six stages to a finished piece with working lighting, a stone fountain, moss beds, and painted details. Taking photos at each stage helped me track how far the build had come, and made it much easier to see what was still missing before I could actually call it done.
